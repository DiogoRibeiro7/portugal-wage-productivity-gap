# Article map

## Working title

**Is Portugal's Wage Gap Larger Than Its Productivity Gap? Productivity, Wage Pass-Through and the Portuguese Pay Residual**

## 1. Motivation

Start from a narrow empirical observation rather than a political claim: Portugal has lower labour productivity than richer European economies, but the proportional shortfall in labour compensation may be larger than the proportional shortfall in productivity.

The paper's contribution is to quantify that difference using matched definitions and then ask whether Portugal remains a low-pay outlier conditional on productivity.

## 2. Measurement before explanation

The paper should make the denominator problem explicit. “Average salary” and “productivity” can refer to incompatible objects. The first table should therefore state exactly:

- compensation concept;
- labour-input denominator;
- price conversion;
- benchmark;
- source;
- year.

This section is important because it prevents an attractive but invalid comparison between unrelated OECD/Eurostat aggregates.

## 3. The descriptive gap

Main figure: Portuguese compensation shortfall and productivity shortfall relative to the EU-27 benchmark, calculated separately for each year in PPS.

Main object:

\[
\delta_t
=\log(W_{PT,t}/W_{EU,t})
-\log(P_{PT,t}/P_{EU,t}).
\]

The paper should report both log gaps and intuitive percentage shortfalls.

## 4. Conditional European benchmark

Fit the wage–productivity relationship on comparator countries, excluding Portugal. Predict Portugal only after estimation.

Main figure: observed Portuguese compensation against compensation predicted from the comparator-country relationship.

The purpose is not to estimate a structural wage equation. It is to answer a simpler question: *is Portugal paid less than economies with similar productivity would predict?*

## 5. Growth and pass-through

Move from levels to changes using real time-series measures, not PPS levels. Estimate the association between productivity growth and real compensation growth with country and year fixed effects.

This section distinguishes:

\[
\text{low productivity level}
\]

from

\[
\text{weak transmission of productivity gains to labour compensation}.
\]

They are not the same hypothesis.

## 6. Where can the residual come from?

Mechanism analysis should be sequential rather than kitchen-sink regression. Candidate blocks:

1. labour share / unit labour costs;
2. capital intensity and sector composition;
3. firm size and productivity dispersion;
4. bargaining institutions and non-standard contracts;
5. minimum wage;
6. taxation and employer social contributions.

Each block should show how much the Portuguese residual changes when added. A disappearing residual is informative even without a causal interpretation.

## 7. Taxation decomposition

Keep three concepts separate:

\[
\text{employer labour cost}
\rightarrow
\text{gross employee pay}
\rightarrow
\text{net employee income}.
\]

A tax-wedge explanation can affect the transitions, but it cannot explain a compensation shortfall that is already present at the employer-cost stage. This gives the paper a clean way to test fiscal explanations without arguing from headline tax rates.

## 8. Robustness

Registered checks:

- per-person versus per-hour measures;
- EU aggregate versus country median;
- euro-area-only benchmark;
- Southern Europe benchmark;
- excluding Ireland and Luxembourg because of known national-accounts level distortions;
- pre/post euro-crisis and pre/post pandemic windows;
- AMECO versus Eurostat real-growth series;
- provider-vintage sensitivity where feasible.

## 9. Conclusion

The conclusion should be conditional on the result. If productivity explains most of the Portuguese wage shortfall, say so. If a large residual remains, the contribution is to establish its magnitude and narrow the set of plausible explanations—not to assign the residual mechanically to capital, taxes, or bargaining power.
